Case study

Clinician dashboard

Giving clinicians interpretation, not just data.

A clinical decision support dashboard that turns 28 days of cycle data into a reading a clinician can trust in the three minutes they actually have.

3–5 min

is all a clinician gets to read a patient’s full cycle and decide what matters

15–20 patients reviewed daily · the design constraint behind every decision below
02 The ask

Design for a clinician mid-consultation

I was asked to design a dashboard for clinicians reviewing a patient’s menstrual cycle data — one that represents data across the cycle, highlights patterns and relationships, and supports fast clinical interpretation.

A clinician is reviewing a patient’s cycle data mid-consultation. With limited time, they need to spot abnormalities, understand the pattern, and decide whether anything needs further evaluation.

What the dashboard had to do
  1. 01Represent data across the full cycle
  2. 02Highlight patterns and relationships
  3. 03Enable fast clinical interpretation
  4. 04Surface important events
03 Grounding the problem

No clinicians to interview — so I built the case from evidence instead

I didn’t have access to clinicians to validate this directly. The problem understanding instead came from three sources:

Clinical resources

Established patterns on menstrual cycle physiology — what actually matters medically, like LH timing and BBT shifts.

Comparative app audit

How existing health-tracking apps do — or mostly don’t — support clinical interpretation rather than patient self-tracking.

Cognitive load research

UX research on data visualization, to understand what makes multi-variable time-series data slow to parse under time pressure.

  • H1Data is difficult to interpret quickly — raw values don’t say what’s normal versus worth flagging.
  • H2Important insights aren’t visible upfront; the clinician has to dig for the one thing that matters.
  • H3Graphs create high cognitive load, with several overlapping variables and no guided reading order.

These are informed hypotheses built from research, not clinician-verified findings — a distinction I address directly further down.

06 The solution

Surfaces the one thing that matters — before anything else.

Clarity over complexity — urgency, understanding, then action, in one view

Rather than adding more visualisations, the dashboard restructures what’s shown and in what order — moving the clinician through a single scroll instead of five disconnected panels.

Prototype not loading? Open it in Figma

Eight decisions built this screen, in a deliberate order: urgency, then understanding, then action. Open one to see where it lives.

The single most urgent finding, in one plain sentence, placed first so nothing is missed.

Is there anything urgent?

Name, age, cycle length, date range — nothing else. Three seconds to orient.

Who am I looking at?

Four one-line insights with a consistent colour code carried across the whole dashboard.

What is this cycle’s story?

Red for act now, amber for monitor — separated by severity, not chronology.

How many issues, how serious?

Pain/Energy and BBT/LH kept separate rather than overlaid — a direct answer to cognitive load.

What are the patterns?

Seven values for quick reference, with the abnormal LH→BBT gap highlighted.

What are the exact values?

Menstrual, Follicular, Ovulation, Luteal — each framed as a one-line insight, not a raw range.

What’s the story, phase by phase?

Four explicit next actions, each linked back to a specific finding above.

07 The UX work

The screen is the output. The decisions are the work.

Most of this project happened before anything was drawn — in deciding what the clinician needed to be told, in what order, and what to leave out. Here is the method, and the four calls that shaped the layout.

  1. Frame

    Turn a brief into constraints

    Four objectives, and one number that governed everything after it: three to five minutes per patient.

  2. Research

    Build an evidence base without users

    Clinical literature, a competitive audit and cognitive-load research — stated as three hypotheses, not findings.

  3. Define

    Fix a single user in place

    Dr. Reena Shah: fifteen to twenty patients a day, raw tables, three questions she needs answered fast.

  4. Map

    Find where the time actually goes

    Five stages, five friction points — and the discovery that the cost sat between the panels, not inside them.

  5. Structure

    Order before interface

    Urgency, context, pattern, meaning, action — settled as a reading order before any layout existed.

01

Two charts, not one

Considered
Overlaying BBT, LH, pain and energy on a single chart — fewer elements on screen.
Chose
Two charts, each pairing variables that actually relate to one another.
Because
Fewer elements is not the same as less work. The third hypothesis was cognitive load from overlapping variables with no reading order — one chart would have concentrated that, not solved it.
02

One scroll, not five panels

Considered
Keeping the familiar five-panel structure and improving each panel on its own terms.
Chose
A single scroll with a fixed order.
Because
The journey map showed the cost wasn’t inside any one panel — it was in switching between them and holding the thread. Better panels would have left that untouched.
03

Severity, not chronology

Considered
Listing alerts in the order the events occurred across the cycle.
Chose
Splitting them into act now and monitor.
Because
In a three-minute review the first question is “is anything urgent”, not “what happened when”. Chronology is the data’s order; severity is the clinician’s.
04

Insights, not ranges

Considered
Showing each phase’s values and letting the clinician read them.
Chose
One interpreted sentence per phase, worded as indicative rather than diagnostic.
Because
The brief asked for fast clinical interpretation. Handing back raw numbers hands back the work — but the hedge matters just as much, so it supports her judgment instead of replacing it.
08 Why this order

Progressive disclosure — layers, not everything at once

The order is the design. Each layer only makes sense once the one before it has landed.

1Urgency

Critical information sits at the top so action isn’t missed.

2Context

Patient details and summary appear early, before the data itself.

3Pattern

Graphs come after summary, once there’s a frame to read them against.

4Meaning

Phase cards interpret the data, cutting the need for manual reasoning.

5Action

Clinical direction closes the view, guiding the actual decision.

09 What I’d do differently

Naming the gap, rather than hoping no one asks

This was built entirely from desk research, without direct access to clinicians. That’s a real constraint worth stating plainly rather than glossing over.

  • 01

    Validate the three hypotheses

    Interpretation difficulty, low visibility of key insights, and cognitive load are informed assumptions — not yet confirmed by practising clinicians.

  • 02

    Test under real time pressure

    The 3–5 minute review window shaped the whole hierarchy. I’ve designed for it, but haven’t observed it hold up in a live consultation.

  • 03

    Check the clinical language with a gynaecologist

    “Indicative, not diagnostic” is a deliberate safety choice — its exact wording needs clinical sign-off, not just my judgment.

Designed to work, not impress.

Every colour, every section, every word exists because somewhere a clinician has three minutes, fifteen patients, and one question:

What does this data mean for this patient, right now?

It takes five variables across twenty-eight days and turns them into a story a clinician can read, act on immediately, and return to with confidence — without changing the mental model they already work in.
